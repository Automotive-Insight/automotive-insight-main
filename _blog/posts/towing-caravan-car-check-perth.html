The van's packed and the route is sorted. Maybe it's up the Brand Highway to Kalbarri, maybe out to Esperance. The car that does the school run without a fuss is about to haul nearly two tonnes through 40-degree afternoons, and towing has a way of finding weak spots that ordinary driving never touches. A check before you go is cheap insurance.

Key takeaways

  • Know your towing capacity, tow ball limit and GVM. You can be fine on one and over on another.
  • The towbar and coupling need to be rated for the loaded weight of the van.
  • Brakes, suspension, tyres and cooling take the most strain when you tow.
  • Vans over 750 kg need their own brakes, and heavier vans need more.
  • Book a pre-tow check a week or two before you leave.

Start with the numbers

Every car, towbar and caravan comes with limits from its maker: towing capacity, tow ball download, gross vehicle mass (GVM) and gross combination mass. You'll find your car's figures in the owner's manual and on the compliance plate, and the caravan's on its own plate.

The one that catches people out is GVM. A family SUV can sit comfortably under its towing limit and still be over its GVM once the family, the luggage and the tow ball weight are all on board. Some popular SUVs have surprisingly low limits. A 2019 RAV4 Hybrid AWD, for instance, is rated to tow 1,500 kg, with a 150 kg tow ball limit. If you're unsure, take the loaded rig across a public weighbridge. Transport WA's towing page is a good place to read up on the rules.

Towbar, coupling and chains

The towbar and coupling have to be rated for at least the loaded weight of your van. We check the rating, look for rust or cracks around the mounts, check the ball for wear and inspect the safety chain points. Under WA rules, a trailer below 2,500 kg ATM needs at least one safety chain, and from 2,500 kg up it needs two.

Brakes and suspension

Two extra tonnes make your brakes work much harder, especially on long downhill runs like Greenmount Hill on the way back into Perth. We check the pads, discs and brake fluid. Brake fluid absorbs moisture as it ages, and old fluid can boil under long, heavy braking. That's when the pedal starts to feel soft.

At the back, tired springs let the car squat under the tow ball weight. The front lifts, the steering goes light and your headlights point into the eyes of oncoming drivers. Worn shock absorbers make sway harder to control, too. Our brakes and suspension work covers all of this.

The van has its own rules. In WA, any trailer of 750 kg GTM or more has to have brakes, and once it's over 2,000 kg those brakes must work on every wheel, with a system that applies them if the van ever comes off the towbar. If your van has electric brakes, make sure the controller in the car is working and set for the load.

Tyres, and both spares

Check the tread, look for cracks or bulges, and read the date stamp on the sidewall, because tyres harden with age even when there's plenty of tread left. Set the pressures to the towing or full-load figures on your tyre placard. Then do the same for both spares, the car's and the van's. Discovering a flat spare 300 km from the nearest town is not how you want to spend an afternoon.

Cooling and the gearbox

Towing in WA heat keeps the cooling system under load for hours at a time, so we check the coolant, hoses, radiator and fans. Automatic gearboxes run hotter when towing too, and many logbooks list shorter service intervals for cars that tow. If your next logbook service is due soon, it makes sense to get it done before the trip.

Lights and wiring

Hook the van up and test everything: tail lights, brake lights, both indicators and reverse. Flickering lights usually mean corroded pins in the trailer plug. If you've got a DC-DC charger or Anderson plug running to the van's battery, we'll check that wiring as well.

What our pre-tow inspection covers

We go over the car and everything that connects it to the van: towbar and coupling, brakes, suspension, tyres and spares, cooling, gearbox, trailer plug and lights. Afterwards you get a written summary that separates what needs fixing before you leave from what can wait until you're home.

Once you're on the road, WA's rule is the lesser of the posted limit or 100 km/h when you're towing. RAC WA suggests sitting between 80 and 90 km/h for better braking and handling.

When should I book my pre-tow inspection?

One to two weeks before you leave. That gives us time to order parts if something turns up.

Where do I find my car's towing capacity?

In the owner's manual and on the compliance plate. Check the tow ball limit and GVM while you're there.

Do I need trailer brakes in WA?

Yes. In WA, trailers of 750 kg GTM or more must have brakes, and your car's unbraked limit is capped at 750 kg or the maker's figure, whichever is lower.

How fast can I go when towing a caravan in WA?

The lesser of the posted limit or 100 km/h. RAC WA recommends 80 to 90 km/h.

Should I get my car serviced before a big towing trip?

If a service is due soon, yes. Get it done before you go.
