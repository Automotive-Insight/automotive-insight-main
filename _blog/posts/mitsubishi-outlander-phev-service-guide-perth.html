For a lot of Australians, the Outlander PHEV was their first plug-in car. It landed here in late 2014, when there wasn't much else like it, and it's been a familiar sight on Perth roads ever since. That means plenty of them are now well past their new-car warranty, often in their second or third home. If yours is one of them, this is what's worth knowing.

Key takeaways

  • Battery sizes changed a lot between models, from 12 kWh on early cars to 20 kWh from 2022.
  • Older cars most often lose battery capacity, and heat makes it worse.
  • A tired or incorrect 12-volt battery can stop the car starting.
  • Mitsubishi's 10-year warranty needs dealer servicing, but the standard warranty doesn't.
  • Service it every 12 months or 15,000 km.

How the Outlander PHEV has changed

The first generation ran from 2014 to 2021. It had a 2.0-litre petrol engine, a 12 kWh battery and an electric motor at each end for all-wheel drive, with a claimed electric range of 54 km. Later cars switched to a 2.4-litre engine and a 13.8 kWh battery. These first-generation cars charge through a Type 1 socket, and most variants also have a CHAdeMO fast-charging port.

The second generation arrived in 2022 and was a big step up. It kept a 2.4-litre engine but paired it with more powerful motors, for 185 kW and 450 Nm. The battery grew to 20 kWh, and the claimed electric range rose to 84 km. It can also power your home or feed electricity back to the grid, and it's rated to tow 1,600 kg. Fast charging still uses a CHAdeMO port.

A 2026 update brought an even bigger 22.7 kWh battery, good for a claimed 86 km of electric driving.

Problems owners run into

The one most first-generation owners notice is a battery that doesn't go as far as it used to. Every battery loses some capacity with age, and Perth's summer heat speeds things up. Repair costs vary enormously. Carsales has quoted anywhere from around $500 to replace individual cells to about $13,000 for a new pack, at 2023 prices. A capacity test is the sensible first step, because it tells you where the battery really stands before you spend anything.

The small 12-volt battery in the back of the luggage area causes its share of trouble too. The car needs it to start up, so if it's weak, the Outlander may refuse to go into Ready even with a fully charged main battery. It also has to be the right kind. Recall REC-001686 covered 3,677 Outlander PHEVs built between 2013 and 2020 that may have had the original AGM battery replaced with a different type.

Brakes are the other thing to watch. Because the electric motors do so much of the slowing, the discs don't get worked hard, and they can rust. In the UK, early first-generation cars were recalled because water could get past the parking brake caliper boot. On older cars, we check the whole braking system, parking brake included.

There have been a few other recalls. REC-001055 covered a second-row seatbelt anchor on 1,948 cars from 2019 and 2020, and REC-005692 fixed a reversing camera software fault on 22MY and 23MY Outlanders. You can check any car's VIN at vehiclerecalls.gov.au.

What we check at a service

Outlander PHEVs are due every 12 months or 15,000 km. Our hybrid servicing covers the oil, filter and fluids to Mitsubishi's specification, a drive battery health and capacity test, the regenerative braking, a high-voltage safety check, and the coolant and inverter system. On an Outlander, we also test the 12-volt battery and go over the charging system.

If you mostly drive on electricity, don't be tempted to stretch the service because the kilometres are low. The engine oil still ages, so the 12-month interval still applies. And if you're new to plug-in hybrids, our PHEV servicing guide covers the basics.

The battery warranty

For Outlander PHEVs bought after 1 October 2020, Mitsubishi covers the drive battery for eight years or 160,000 km. The 10-year, 200,000 km warranty on the rest of the car is a different matter. It only applies if every scheduled service is done at a Mitsubishi dealer. Otherwise, the standard five-year, 100,000 km warranty applies, as it does for cars bought before October 2020. From the 22MY model onwards, Mitsubishi also covers the battery if its usable capacity drops below 66 per cent.

Servicing with an independent workshop doesn't void the standard warranty, but it does end that extended 10-year cover. If you're thinking of leaving the dealer network, ask Mitsubishi to confirm in writing how your battery cover will work.

Charging checks

A lot of charging faults are simple fixes that get put off. We check the charge port and pins for heat marks, corrosion or damage, look over the charging cable, make sure the car starts and stops charging properly, and scan for any stored faults. One tip: before buying a wall charger or a spare cable, confirm which plug type your generation uses.

When not to wait

Some things shouldn't wait for the next service. That includes an EV system warning, a sudden drop in electric range, a car that won't go into Ready, charging that stops halfway, or an engine that seems to run far more than it used to. Our EV diagnostics dig deeper than reading fault codes.

What goes wrong most often on an Outlander PHEV?

Battery capacity loss on older cars, 12-volt battery problems and rusty brakes from underuse.

Will servicing my Outlander PHEV outside Mitsubishi void the warranty?

Your standard warranty stays in place. The extended 10-year cover needs dealer servicing.

How long do Outlander PHEV batteries last?

It depends on the car's age, the heat it's lived in and how it's been charged. A capacity test will tell you how yours is doing.

How often should it be serviced?

Every 12 months or 15,000 km, whichever comes first.

Can I charge it from a normal power point?

Yes. A first-generation car takes around five hours to charge from a household socket. A wall charger is faster.
